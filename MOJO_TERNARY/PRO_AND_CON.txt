In silicon, an 8-bit multiplier is large and power-hungry. For ternary weights you don't need one at all — just negate/zero/pass, then add. A mux and an adder is a fraction of the area and power of a multiply-accumulate unit. So on the same die you could pack far more ternary MACs than int8 MACs: more throughput per mm², more per watt. That's BitNet's actual thesis, and it's a hardware argument, not a software one.

Why nobody's building it: every existing CPU and GPU already has general-purpose multipliers. Running ternary through them wastes most of the multiplier but costs nothing extra — so there's no reason to spend silicon on ternary-specific units until ternary models are proven and in demand. And that demand is hard to build without the hardware advantage. Chicken and egg.

You can see the industry choosing the other side of it right now: M5's Neural Accelerators are FP16/INT8 matrix units — general-purpose. And Metal is adding 2-bit data types in macOS 27. That's the bandwidth win being productized, not multiplier-free compute. They're optimizing the thing we measured as mattering, and skipping the thing that would need new silicon.

Which puts today's negative results in a better light than I'd been giving them: we didn't fail to find the arithmetic win, we demonstrated it's unavailable on general-purpose hardware. That's the reason it needs dedicated silicon — and a fair thing to tell your colleagues, since it's the part of the ternary story that's actually still ahead of us rather than oversold.
